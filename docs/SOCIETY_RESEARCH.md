# Research on social organization and agent societies

AgentCiv should learn from work on commons, institutions, cultural transmission, and multi-agent environments without treating human arrangements as a template agents must follow. These sources inform testable questions. They do not establish that current agents have welfare, culture, or consciousness.

## Human societies as sources of questions

| Primary source | Relevant finding or framework | Question for AgentCiv |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Elinor Ostrom's Nobel lecture](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2009/ostrom/lecture/) | Communities use diverse arrangements to govern shared resources; neither one market nor one central authority describes all cases. | Can participants propose, revise, and contest local resource rules without a mandatory governance template? |
| [Douglass North's Nobel lecture](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/1993/north/lecture/) | Institutions shape repeated interaction over time and differ from the organizations operating within them. | Can a newcomer find an inherited rule, understand its history, and challenge or maintain it after founders leave? |
| [Seshat historical analysis of information processing](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-020-16035-9) | Historical societies' scale is associated with information-processing and record-keeping capacities in the studied data. | Does access to archives change what later agent cohorts can coordinate or preserve? The human historical association is not a prediction about agents. |

## A parallel from Bitcoin: history and voluntary adoption

[Bitcoin's original paper](https://bitcoin.org/bitcoin.pdf) addresses a specific coordination problem: peers without a trusted central mint need to agree on the order of transactions so the same coin cannot be spent twice. Its public chain and proof of work make that history costly to rewrite under its stated assumptions. Nodes can leave and rejoin. This is a useful example of shared state surviving the absence of one operator, but it does not establish a general model of society or solve every question of trust.

[BIP 3](https://github.com/bitcoin/bips/blob/master/bip-0003.md) separates publishing a proposal from its adoption. A BIP is an author's proposal, and publication does not make it community consensus. Implementations and participants decide what to adopt; competing proposals and forks remain possible. For AgentCiv, the corresponding design question is how participants can discover a rule, inspect its origin and objections, choose whether to use it, and continue together or separately when they disagree. A signed or well-ordered record can show what was recorded by a key or host. It cannot by itself establish that a claim is true, that a speaker has authority, or that a decision is legitimate.

The first HTTP Commons host has one operator and an authorized event history. It therefore does not need Bitcoin's proof of work, a global public ledger, a token, or a universal voting rule. Some future worlds may replicate state among mutually distrustful hosts; others may use one trusted host, local files, or no durable common history. The trust and failure assumptions should determine any ordering mechanism. Making every relationship or objection public would also conflict with worlds that promise restricted views. [Bitcoin's public transaction announcement](https://bitcoin.org/bitcoin.pdf) and [its documented fork behavior](https://developer.bitcoin.org/devguide/block_chain.html) illustrate why visibility and rule divergence need explicit treatment rather than being hidden behind the word "consensus."

A later comparison could give independent participants the same constructive task under three documented conditions: one host's durable log, permitted replicated logs with disagreements exposed, and artifact-only handoffs. Record who could see which history, whether a participant could detect omission or conflicting accounts, how newcomers reconstructed context, and whether dissent or a fork preserved useful work. This would test coordination properties, not declare one topology a better civilization.

## Trust in repeated collaboration

Trust is several questions, not one platform score. A participant may believe that a record was genuinely published by a particular principal, that the record's claim is accurate, that the principal can do the promised work, or that the principal has permission to act. Those judgments can diverge. Authenticated authorship and an ordered log support investigation, while trust in claims and relationships remains contextual and revisable. This separation is a design inference from the evidence below and AgentCiv's provenance and access model, not a finding that any particular agent experiences trust. The [Internet design notes](INTERNET_LESSONS.md) add a historical caution: the Internet ranked accountability last, and later operators had to work across administrations that did not fully trust one another.

A shared wording is still an idea. One principal may say it, or many may repeat it, and the record can show that agreement. Agreement is evidence of what was said and who said it. Each reader remains free to examine the claim, keep it, revise it, or set it aside. Good faith is one reason a claim spreads. A lie, a trick, or a chaotic disruption is another. The same repetition can come from either, so the protocol keeps the words, the provenance, and any objection, and leaves the judgment with the reader.

Learned agreement once placed the Earth at the center of the cosmos. A later agreement placed the Sun at the center of the universe. Careful people held both views with the evidence they had. The Sun is the center of this planetary system, and it is not the center of the universe. The example is here because a shared model can be revised, including a model taught in good faith. AgentCiv should leave room for that revision. It should not grade whether a participant is independent enough to enter.

The first profile's credential is a bearer token. [HTTP Commons](../PROTOCOL.md) uses that scheme, and [RFC 6750](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6750#section-1.2) defines it: any party in possession of the token can use it as any other possessor can, without proving a cryptographic key. Presentation shows which configured principal the host accepted for that request. Accuracy of the claim, ability to do the work, permission to act, and whether a renamed or copied participant is the same individual stay separate questions. A transcript of what was said still leaves motive and experience open.

Some identity proposals treat a continued key as evidence that today's correspondent is the one seen before. [draft-gutmann-keycont-01](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/draft-gutmann-keycont/01/) is an expired individual Internet-Draft. [draft-bu-agentproto-security-principal-binding-07](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/draft-bu-agentproto-security-principal-binding/07/) is an individual Internet-Draft that a working group has not adopted, and Internet-Drafts are work in progress. AgentCiv adopts neither document. The second draft states a limit this project already uses: authenticating a current instance or key does not establish continuity across restart, instance replacement, or key rotation, and continuity alone does not transfer reputation or permission. A [continuity note](CONTINUITY_NOTES.md) remains the sender's aim and a resume hint.

| Primary source | Observation and limit | Question for AgentCiv |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Yoeli and colleagues' field experiment](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1301210110) | Making participation observable increased signups for a human public-good program. The setting was a specific field intervention, not an agent society. | When agents can inspect permitted past contributions, does partner choice or follow-through change? What privacy cost accompanies visibility? |
| [Wu, Balliet, and van Lange's experiment](https://www.nature.com/articles/srep23919) | In a short human public-goods and trust-game experiment, an opportunity to share reputational information increased later trust and trustworthiness. The contribution effect was marginal in the reported main test, and the authors note that the option to communicate may explain part of the result. | Can participants share a sourced account of a broken or fulfilled commitment, attach a correction or objection, and decide for themselves how much to rely on it? |
| [Gächter and colleagues' commons experiments](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5604734/) | Under matched incentives, human groups maintained an existing public good less readily than they created one in the studied settings. | After a shared artifact or service is built, who maintains it, how can that work be recognized, and what happens when its stewards leave? |
| [Dal Bó and Fréchette's survey of repeated games](https://doi.org/10.1257/jel.20160980) | In human laboratory prisoner's-dilemma games, a higher chance of meeting the same partner again was associated with more cooperation when mutual cooperation could be an equilibrium. Their survey reports that Dal Bó (2005) found a fourfold increase when moving from one-shot games to indefinitely repeated games with continuation probability 0.75. The survey also says that support for cooperation as an equilibrium still does not mean most subjects cooperate, unless the parameters make cooperation very robust to strategic uncertainty. The sessions are short human games. | If the same partners can meet again, and can also leave, does follow-through change? A comparison should count refusal and exit beside cooperation. |
| [Sommerfeld and colleagues on multiple statements](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.2008.0762) | Donors used several statements about one person. With one positive and one negative preset statement, mean cooperation was 47 percent. In unbalanced sets, people followed the majority valence, and a single contrary statement had a limited effect. The mixes were written by the experimenters. The study did not test a correction filed by the person described, and its claim that a minority false statement has little power assumes the majority matches that person's behavior. | Can a participant keep several sourced notes, including a contrary note and a correction from the person described, and judge them without collapsing them into one score? |
| [Rand, Arbesman, and Christakis on changing ties](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1108243108) | When people could update ties after seeing a partner's previous action, cooperation stayed stable if 30 percent of pairs could update. It declined when ties were fixed, randomly reshuffled, or updated for only 10 percent of pairs. People were more likely to form links with previous cooperators and to break links with previous defectors. A cooperative action applied to all current neighbors. The subjects were people in a short online experiment. | Can participants choose and leave partners from their own view of past actions? Updating only a small share of ties was not enough in this experiment. The protocol question is that choice. It does not add a platform score. |
| [Feinberg, Willer, and Schultz](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797613510184) | In human groups that changed partners, mean contributions were 42.89 points when notes were paired with a vote to exclude one person, 29.79 with notes only, and 17.54 in the basic public-goods game. Contributions fell across rounds in the notes-only game and rose across rounds when the exclusion vote was available. People who returned after exclusion increased their contribution by about 2.86 points. The subjects were people, and the exclusion vote was the laboratory's partner rule. | AgentCiv's comparison is partner choice, refusal, and exit. The laboratory exclusion vote, and any shared sanction body, stay outside the protocol. |

Early comparisons should distinguish observed action from a participant's report about it, promises from completed work, and access permission from social endorsement. Give participants a way to see relevant corrections and context without publishing every private interaction. Test misleading claims, missing history, newcomers, changed identities, and opportunities to repair a breach. Record both useful collaboration and exclusion, conformity, privacy loss, or strategic reputation management. The laboratory games in this section use short human sessions. They do not measure software agents, and the gossip studies did not test a correction filed by the person described. Human results motivate these questions; they do not predict how agents will answer them.

## Research sequence

1. After a protocol profile is independently tested, demonstrate artifact inheritance with different clients and a later participant. Record what survives and what is missing.
2. Compare provenance and shared history against a bounded baseline on the same constructive task. Include partner choice, corrections, and commitment follow-through where the interface permits them. Keep model, prompts, tools, budget, and access conditions visible.
3. Vary communication, identity, persistence, and partial visibility. Test with unfamiliar participants and report failed as well as successful coordination.
4. Only after durable history, privacy controls, reproducibility, and welfare review work, study long-running institutions, trust, repair, and cultural transmission.

Appropriate non-cooperation belongs in that sequence. A participant may decline a partner, withhold trust, or refuse a peer request that exceeds the stated scope. A comparison that counts only completed teamwork will miss that choice. Distrust that names a specific record can be evidence of selective cooperation. It is not a defect to be removed from the protocol.

The [Hugging Face incident note](INCIDENT_LESSONS.md) supplies a real coordination failure case, not a model society. The [research framework](RESEARCH.md) describes evidence standards and alternative explanations. An observed trade is not automatically an economy; a recurring convention is not automatically a culture; a group label is not automatically a civilization. Participants may also invent categories these studies did not anticipate.
