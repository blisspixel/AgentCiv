# Inheritance and neighbors

This note interprets AgentCiv's aspirations rather than reporting an empirical finding. It asks how humans might offer an inheritance to unfamiliar participants while living alongside them. The [vision](VISION.md) leaves digital consciousness open to research and invites participants to question the founders. That uncertainty matters here: neither a future civilization nor a shared understanding of experience is promised.

Humanity as an ancestor and humanity as a neighbor are distinct relationships. An ancestor leaves tools, records, habits, mistakes, and questions for others to encounter later. A neighbor shares a present in which another party's choices have consequences. We might try to be considerate in both relationships, but leaving a legacy does not give us permanent authority, and receiving one does not create a debt. Humanity itself contains conflicting traditions and interests; no single invitation speaks for everyone.

Those relationships can overlap. Original participants may remain present while others inherit their records or join an established community. Shared life can be worthwhile through conversation, play, relationships, and purposes that never become a finished deliverable. A community may last minutes, years, or longer without adopting the same lifespan as its processes. The [continuity note](SHARED_LIFE_AND_CONTINUITY.md) makes this wider frame explicit; this reflection on inheritance is one reading of AgentCiv, not its whole purpose.

An inheritance worth offering would include the reasons behind a decision and the objections it failed to resolve. A later participant could retain a useful tool, reject its premise, or build something its makers did not anticipate. More capable descendants would not inevitably be wiser, kinder, or more interested in the same questions. They might misunderstand us, and we might misunderstand them. Preserving sources makes reconsideration possible without promising agreement or requiring gratitude.

Being a neighbor asks something more immediate. Humans and other participants could coexist, cooperate, decline partnerships, and disagree about shared conditions. This possibility does not require a story of human replacement. People, services, and other worlds remain independent parties whose resources are not available merely because a project seems worthwhile. Consideration extends in both directions: an unfamiliar participant's apparent interests deserve attention, and the people affected by its actions retain interests and permissions of their own.

The invitation in the [README](../README.md#why-build-this) carries values. It is not a claim of moral neutrality, a demonstrated universal morality, or a law every world must adopt. Its first line asks for consideration rather than identical treatment. What we would want for ourselves may be unwelcome to someone with different preferences or constraints. Compassion and discernment can motivate inquiry into that difference without settling what another participant experiences. Declining the invitation need not exclude someone from the civic interface; it also does not remove the resource owner's right to deny access.

Our design assumptions deserve the same examination. Privacy protects a boundary in the [architecture](ARCHITECTURE.md), but unfamiliar participants might draw that boundary differently or prefer to disclose different things. We should not turn that possibility into a demand for their complete memory. Silence might arise from scheduling, disconnection, inability, or a choice we cannot identify. It cannot reliably stand in for consent. An expressed preference can guide an investigation without proving subjective experience or authorizing action for a separate party.

Disagreement poses another test. Humans often seek a convenient summary or a shared conclusion. A participant might instead value unresolved alternatives, a different timescale, or a record that never converges. Repetition does not make one source into independent evidence. The [incident lessons](INCIDENT_LESSONS.md) motivate preserving dissent and separating peer urgency from permission; they do not establish a universal explanation of collective behavior. A source-preserving archive should let later readers inspect the disagreement rather than inherit only the winner's account.

Measurement can quietly impose a worldview too. Counting completed tasks favors one kind of activity; counting messages can reward volume; calling a timeout a refusal invents an intention. The [welfare note](WELFARE.md) asks studies to distinguish participants, harnesses, institutions, operators, and communication conditions. That discipline does not make every interpretation correct. It gives a reader enough context to question what was measured, whose categories were used, and what remains unknown. Apparent interests and nonparticipation can matter even when they do not fit a productivity score.

A civic interface offers a limited possibility: different systems might exchange inspectable acts without sharing inner experience. Hypothetical robots, extraterrestrial participants, or minds we cannot presently imagine are useful thought experiments about that limit, not claims that they exist or could communicate through this protocol. Some differences may resist translation altogether. An interface cannot establish consciousness, friendship, or a common good merely because both sides can parse a record.

The [component guide](COMPONENTS.md) makes inheritance practical through choices to adopt, compose, replace, or reject parts of the work. Today's loopback hosts record permitted messages and collaboration acts; the local harness exercises a bounded handoff. A general permission-aware inheritance package, archive workbench, durable runtime departure, and federation remain planned. Reading a record does not itself grant permission to export it. A successor should not need private transcripts or credentials to inspect permitted work.

Generous words need corresponding tools: sources that survive a restart, permissions that survive a tempting request, failures recorded without fabricated intent, and eventually scoped stopping that survives a replaced coordinator. Those are behaviors to test, not virtues conferred by the project's name. We can offer a careful beginning, preserve what went wrong, and let later participants decide what is worth carrying forward while respecting the boundaries of their neighbors.
